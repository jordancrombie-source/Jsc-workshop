A plain guide for business owners

Links you cannot see

Someone may have put links, pages, or thousands of junk categories on your website that visitors never see, but search engines do. Here is how to check, what it costs you, and how to put it right, one click at a time.

Written by Jordan Crombie, JSC Workshop, Bilston. Free to copy and pass on. Last reviewed 22 August 2026.

This page prints cleanly, so you can print it or save it as a PDF and hand it to whoever looks after your site.

What has happened

Links to other people's websites have been added to your pages, usually gambling or pharmacy sites. They are styled so that nothing appears on screen. Your website looks completely normal to you and to your customers. A heavier version of the same thing adds whole pages to your website, hundreds of them, wearing your logo and your menu.

It does not always look like that, and this is the part that catches people out. On some sites nothing is added to your posts or your pages at all. Instead the categories and tags get filled up, by the thousand, and WordPress publishes a web address for every one of them. On others a page you already had is quietly rewritten, so the address on your business card still works but what is on it is not yours. The checks below look in all of those places, because looking in only one of them is how this goes unnoticed for years.

Search engines do not look at the screen. They read the code behind the page, and there the links are perfectly visible. That is the whole point of it. Whoever did this is being paid to place links on real, established business websites, because a link from a genuine local firm is worth something to them.

Nobody picked you. This is done by software that works its way across the internet looking for sites running an out of date part, and it happens to thousands of small business websites. It is not a reflection on you, and it is not a reflection on whoever built your site.

Check it yourself

Do not take anyone's word for this, including mine. You can see it with your own eyes, you do not need to install anything, and the first check takes about a minute.

There are four checks below. You only need one of them to find something. They look in different places because this is not one single fault: there are several kinds, and a kind that hides from one check will often walk straight past another. If you have ten minutes, do all four.

Start with this one. It is the only check that works on every kind, it needs no login, and it reads your own file rather than anybody else's opinion of your site.

The sitemap check

  1. Open this in a browser, putting your own address in:
    yourbusiness.co.uk/sitemap.xml
    On WordPress it may be yourbusiness.co.uk/wp-sitemap.xml instead. Try both. If neither opens, skip to the next check.
  2. You will see a plain list of links, which is your website telling search engines every address it has. WordPress splits it up by type: one file for posts, one for pages, one for categories, one for tags.
  3. Look at the numbers before you read anything. Open each file in turn and see how many entries it holds. A small firm's website is tens of things, or low hundreds.
  4. Then hold Ctrl and press F and type casino. Try slot, bet and bonus too.

What gives it away is usually the shape rather than the words. One real site we looked at had three blog posts, sixteen pages, and one thousand eight hundred and thirty eight categories. You do not need to know anything about websites to see that is wrong. Anything in a language you do not publish in is worth a second look as well, and so is a number far larger than the site you recognise.

One caution, and it matters. A sitemap lists what your website advertises, not what it currently serves. Some of these get half cleaned up, so an address can still be listed after the page behind it has gone. Open two or three of the odd ones before you decide how big the problem is.

The second check asks Google directly what it has stored under your name. It is the one that shows you what a customer would find.

The Google check

  1. Go to Google and search for this, putting your own address where the example is:
    site:yourbusiness.co.uk casino
    Type it exactly like that: the word site, a colon, your address with no spaces, then a space, then the word.
  2. Then run it again swapping casino for slots, gambling, and viagra.

If Google shows you pages on your own website about casinos, in any language, that is the problem, and you are looking at exactly what a customer would find. This works on a phone, needs nothing installed, and cannot be fooled by anything hidden. One lone result is worth opening before you worry, because Google can briefly keep showing a page that has since been removed; a pile of them, especially in other languages, is the real thing.

Nothing coming back is not the all clear. Google will not show you a page that has been marked hide from search engines, and it may simply not have got round to a page yet. We have seen a site with well over a thousand junk addresses on it where this search returned nothing at all. Do the sitemap check as well.

The second check looks inside the page itself. It catches links that are hidden rather than published as pages, so it is worth doing as well, but it is fiddlier and one wrong step gives a falsely clean result.

The source check

  1. Open your website in Chrome or Edge, on a computer. This will not work on a phone.
  2. Hold Ctrl and press U. On a Mac, hold Option and Command and press U. A tab of code opens.
    Check the address bar now reads view-source: at the front. If it does not, the next step searches the wrong thing and will wrongly tell you the site is clean.
  3. Hold Ctrl and press F, type casino and press Enter. The browser counts them for you.

A clean page finds nothing. A hit is usually dozens. Run it again with slots, viagra, payday and poker. A caution on short words like bet and spin: they hide inside ordinary English, so "better" matches bet and a booking page may say "book a slot". One or two of those are innocent. Thirty matches naming websites you have never heard of are not.

The look at it properly check

  1. Open your own website the way a customer would, on a phone if that is how most people see it.
  2. Scroll past the top. Look at any list of recent posts or news, and look at the footer.
  3. If your site has a blog or news area, open it.

This sounds too obvious to be worth doing, and it is the one people skip. On some sites the business heading and menu survive while the content underneath has been replaced with casino articles, so the page still looks like yours at a glance. If you have not actually read your own news page this year, read it now.

If any check finds something, do not panic and do not rush. Nothing is on fire. It has most likely been there for months, and an hour spent doing this properly beats ten minutes spent doing it twice. If the checks disagree, believe the one that found something: hidden content is designed to be missed, so finding nothing is never proof on its own.

And if all four come back with nothing. That is genuinely good news and it is the likeliest reading. It is not a guarantee, and I would rather say so than pretend otherwise. There is at least one kind that hides from all four at once. It is a page marked hide from search engines, holding no links of its own, leaving your front page alone, and pulling a casino in from somebody else's server only when somebody opens it. What you can honestly say after these four checks is that the common kinds are not there.

What it actually costs you

  • The one that matters most

    Whoever added the links can still get in

    These attacks almost always leave a way back in, hidden in a file somewhere. Today it is being used for gambling links because that is what pays this month. The same access could be used to read what people send through your contact form, to send your visitors somewhere else, or to lock you out of your own site later. This is why deleting the links on their own does not fix it.

  • Search engines may stop showing you for your own trade

    Google can decide a page it does not trust should not rank, and it can start associating your site with gambling instead of your actual work. It may also put a line under your search result saying the site may be hacked. Customers looking for you may simply not find you.

  • Customers can land on pages that are not yours

    The heavier version of this puts whole articles on your website, in another language, about online casinos, wearing your logo and your menu. Someone checking you out before they book can end up reading one.

  • Your hosting company may take the site down

    Hosts run their own scans, and a site serving spam can be suspended with very little warning. Then the website is gone until it is cleaned up.

Before you fix anything, find out what you control

Three separate things are easy to mistake for one thing, and they can be held by three different people. If someone built your site years ago, some of these may still be in their name.

Two ways to find out, neither of them technical

Search your email for renewal receipts. Registrars and hosting companies send one every year. Whoever's name and card is on those receipts is who controls it. If you cannot find any, someone else is paying, and that is worth knowing on its own.

Try to reset your own password. Go to your website address followed by /wp-admin and click the lost password link. If the reset email arrives in your inbox, you control the site. If it does not, you do not, and that is your answer.

If it turns out the domain is registered to a builder you have lost touch with, deal with that first. It matters more than the links do, because when a domain lapses it takes the website and every address at your own name with it.

How to fix it

In this order, because the order matters. Each step below has a plain description, and under it an exact list of where to click, written for someone who has never been behind their own website. Read the step, then open the clicks.

These steps assume WordPress because most affected sites run it, but this is not a WordPress-only problem. Any site with a login and software behind it can be attacked the same way. If yours is built on something else, the steps still apply in the same order; only the names of the tools change.

The one rule that makes this safe: do step 1 first, and after that a mistake can be undone, because you are holding a copy to fall back on. Be clear about what that copy is: it preserves the site as it stands today, infection and all, so it is a fall-back, not a clean version. That is still exactly what you want before touching anything. And if any step defeats you, stop there. A half-finished fix plus a backup is a perfectly good place to hand over from, and the finding-out is already done.

  1. Take a full backup first

    A backup is a complete copy of the website, downloaded to your own computer, so that a mistake later costs you nothing. Do this before touching anything at all.

    Show me exactly where to click
    1. Log in to your site: go to your website address followed by /wp-admin and sign in. Everything below happens in the menu down the left-hand side.
    2. Click Plugins, then Add New Plugin. In the search box at the top right, type UpdraftPlus.
    3. On the card called UpdraftPlus: WP Backup & Migration Plugin, click Install Now, wait, then click Activate.
    4. Click Settings, then UpdraftPlus Backups. Click the big Backup Now button, leave both ticked boxes as they are, and click Backup Now again. A big site can take a while. That is normal, leave the tab open.
    5. When it finishes, scroll to Existing backups. You will see a row of buttons: Database, Plugins, Themes, Uploads, Others. Click each one in turn, then click Download to your computer. Keep the files somewhere you can find them, and check each download actually finished before moving on.

    If you cannot log in at all, that is not a dead end. Go back a section: the password reset test tells you who can, and your hosting company's support chat can reset access for the account holder. Helping with a hacked site is a normal request for them, they deal with it every day.

  2. Get proper access

    You want two logins before going further: the site's administrator login from step 1, and the login for your hosting control panel. Cleaning up without hosting access is working with one hand.

    Show me exactly where to click
    1. Find your hosting company's name on the renewal receipts in your email, then go to their website and sign in. The lost password link works the same way as anywhere else.
    2. If the account is in someone else's name, ask them to either hand it over or sit with you for an hour. Both are reasonable asks.
    3. While you are talking to your hosting company, ask one more question: what dated backups do they hold? A copy from before the trouble started can shorten the whole job. Do not restore one blindly, because the hole that let this in would come back with it, but knowing it exists is worth a lot.

    Stuck at this step with no route to either login? This is the earliest sensible hand-over point. Everything you have learned so far, who holds what, is exactly what a professional needs on day one.

  3. Remove what was added

    What you are removing depends on which check found it, and there is more than one kind. Fake posts are easy to spot and delete in bulk. Fake categories and tags are just as easy once you know they exist, and they are the ones people miss, because nothing looks wrong under Posts. Hidden links are the hardest to find by hand, and it is completely fine to run the scan in step 4 first and let it point at the file they live in, then come back here.

    Show me exactly where to click

    If you found posts or pages you did not write:

    1. In the left menu click Posts, then All Posts. Fake pages usually sit here as posts you never wrote, often hundreds of them, often about casinos, sometimes in another language. Also check Pages the same way.
    2. Click Screen Options at the very top right, set the number of items to 200, and click Apply, so you can clear them in big batches.
    3. Tick the box at the top of the list to select the whole page of posts, untick anything that is genuinely yours, then open the Bulk actions menu, choose Move to Bin, and click Apply. Repeat until the strangers are gone, then empty the bin.

    If you found hundreds of categories or tags:

    This is the kind that hides in plain sight. Your posts and pages are untouched, so the place you would naturally look is clean. What was added is categories and tags, and WordPress quietly publishes a web address for every one of them.

    1. In the left menu click Posts, then Categories. Not All Posts. Then do the same again under Posts and Tags.
    2. The count sits at the top right of the list. If it reads in the hundreds or thousands and you run a normal small business website, none of that is yours.
    3. Click Screen Options at the very top right, set the number of items to 200, and click Apply.
    4. Tick the box at the top of the list to select the whole page, then untick the handful that are genuinely yours, open Bulk actions, choose Delete, and click Apply. Repeat. Two thousand at two hundred a time is about ten rounds, so put the kettle on.

    Deleting a category does not delete a single one of your posts. This is the bit worth knowing before you start, because nobody sensible bulk deletes two thousand things without it. A post that loses its only category is simply moved to your default one, usually called Uncategorized. Nothing you wrote is lost. You will also find you cannot delete the default category itself, and that is normal.

    Two things that will throw you. First, if you open one of these addresses in a browser it may well say Nothing Found, because there is no article behind it, only the address. That is not proof it has gone: check the list in the admin screens, not the page. Second, if you have someone technical about, the command line tool WP-CLI clears the whole lot in one line and will save you an afternoon. Worth asking before you start clicking.

    If your front page or news page has been taken over:

    1. Your heading and menu are usually still yours, so this is not as bad as it looks. The content underneath has been replaced.
    2. Go to Posts, then All Posts, and clear the strangers as above. On most sites the news area simply shows your latest posts, so removing them restores the page.
    3. Check Posts and then Categories as well. On sites like this we have found the site's own categories renamed to casino names, which is why the menu can read oddly even once the posts are gone.

    If one of your own pages has been rewritten:

    This one is nastier than it sounds, because the address stays the same. A page you hand out on a card still opens, but what is on it is not yours any more. Open Pages, find the page by its name, and look at the revision history in the editor sidebar: you can usually restore the last version you wrote. If there is no earlier version to go back to, that page needs writing again, and the backup in step 1 is the reason you took it.

    If it looks like somebody already half cleaned it:

    If your sitemap lists thousands of addresses but the ones you open give a not found error, someone has removed the pages and left the mess behind. Look at what that error page itself says: if it carries a casino heading rather than your own, the error template was replaced too, and it is still theirs. Treat the site as still compromised and carry on through the rest of these steps. Your host or whoever built the site may have done a partial clean without telling you, so it is worth asking them before you assume the worst.

    If you found hidden links in the code:

    1. Click Pages, open your home page with Edit, and look for a block of links you never added, usually all together in one lump.
    2. If you can see the block, delete it, click Update, and re-run the source check to confirm the count fell to nothing.
    3. If you cannot see it in the editor, stop looking. It is buried in the site's code files, and editing those by hand is the one thing in this guide a beginner should not do. Go to step 4: the scan finds the file for you, and can repair known files with clean official copies.

  4. Find the way back in

    This is the step people skip, and it is why the links come back a fortnight later. Run a security scan across the whole site to find the file that was left behind. Removing the links without closing the door means doing all of this again.
    Show me exactly where to click
    1. Click Plugins, then Add New Plugin, and search for Wordfence. On Wordfence Security, click Install Now, then Activate.
    2. It will ask you to get a free licence. Follow it: enter your email address, and click the link in the email they send. Choose the free option throughout, you do not need the paid one.
    3. In the left menu click Wordfence, then Scan, then Start New Scan. Let it run to the end. This can take a long time on a big site.
    4. Read the results, and save or screenshot them before doing anything else. Lines marked critical that say a file contains malicious code, or an unknown file sits where it should not, are what you are after.
    5. Where Wordfence offers a Repair option, that is the safe button: it replaces a known WordPress, plugin or theme file with an official clean copy. Use it freely.
    6. Do not use the delete-everything button. A scanner cannot always tell malicious code from old or customised code that your site genuinely uses, and deleting everything it flags can break the site. For any file it cannot repair, or anything you do not understand, stop and show the saved report to your hosting company or whoever cleans the site.

    If the results screen frightens you or you are not sure what a line means, stop and hand over here rather than guessing. A finished scan report is genuinely useful work; you have done the diagnosis for them.

  5. Update everything

    WordPress itself, every plugin, and the theme. These attacks get in through out of date parts, so this step is what closes the original hole.

    Show me exactly where to click
    1. In the left menu click Dashboard, then Updates.
    2. If WordPress itself offers an update, run that first and let it finish.
    3. Under plugins, click Select All, then Update Plugins. Do the same for themes.

    A paid theme will quietly stop updating when its licence runs out, which leaves known holes sitting open for years. If your theme shows an update that will not install, or has not updated in years, check whether it is still paid for.

  6. Change every password

    Site administrator, hosting, and any other accounts on the site. Whoever got in may have collected the old ones, so this only counts if it happens after the clean-up, not before.

    Show me exactly where to click
    1. Click Users, then All Users. Look down the list for any administrator you do not recognise. Sometimes one gets added. Delete any stranger.
    2. Click your own name, scroll to Set New Password, click it, and let it generate a strong one. Save it somewhere safe, then click Update Profile.
    3. Change your hosting control panel password the same way, on the hosting company's website.
    4. The site also has behind-the-scenes passwords, called FTP and database passwords, which live in the hosting control panel. If those words mean nothing to you, open your host's support chat and ask them to reset both. It is a routine request and takes them minutes.
    5. One step people miss: before saving the new passwords, run a full antivirus scan on the computer you manage the site from. Stolen passwords often come from the owner's computer rather than the website, and a fresh password typed into an infected machine is stolen again the same day.

    And never send a website or hosting password in an ordinary email, to anyone. If someone helping you needs access, they should have a better way to receive it, and a separate account of their own that you can remove afterwards.

  7. Ask Google to look again

    Google Search Console is Google's own free tool for website owners. It tells you if Google has flagged anything, lets you ask for a fresh look now the spam is gone, and means you hear about it first if anything ever comes back.

    Show me exactly where to click
    1. Search Google for Google Search Console, open it, and sign in with any Google account.
    2. Add your website when it asks. It will then want proof you own the site, and this is the one genuinely technical moment in this guide: it asks you to change a setting at your domain or add a snippet to the site.
    3. If the verification defeats you, skip it with a clear conscience. The site is still fixed without it, and whoever looks after your site can set it up in ten minutes.

Making sure it stays fixed

Here is the part that usually gets left out. Cleaning the site deals with today. It does not deal with why it happened.

These attacks find websites running something out of date. If nothing changes about how the site is looked after, the same scan finds it again, and the whole thing repeats. Someone needs to be applying updates, and that someone has to be a real person with a real login, not a good intention.

So the honest question after the cleanup is a simple one. Who is going to update this, and how often? If the answer is nobody, the fix will not hold.

And a note on what this guide can honestly promise: it helps you confirm the symptom and take the safe first steps. A clean Google result or a finished scan cannot certify that every way back in has gone. The same ground is covered, more formally, in WordPress's own hacked-site guide and Google's guidance for hacked sites, so you do not have to take any of it on my word alone.

When starting again is the better answer

Sometimes cleaning up is the wrong call, and it is worth being straight about when.

If nobody can get into the site and whoever built it has moved on, if it has been cleaned before and it came back, or if it has sat untouched for years and realistically will again, then you are paying to patch something that will keep breaking.

It is also worth asking whether the site needs to work the way it does. A five page site for a trade business is often built on a system designed for publishing several articles a week, and that system is the part that needs constant maintenance. A simpler site with no login and no plugins cannot be broken into this way, because there is nothing to break into, and it costs almost nothing to keep running.

The test is honest and quick. Do you actually change the site yourself? If you update prices or post news most months, you need a system that lets you, and you need somebody maintaining it. If you have not touched it since it was built, you are paying maintenance on a convenience you have never used.
